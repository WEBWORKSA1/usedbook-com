Few things make a used book more personal, or more valuable, than an author's signature. But signatures vary enormously in significance, and the market for them attracts forgeries. Here's how to understand what a signature adds and how to protect yourself.

## Types of signed books

- **Flat signed:** the author's signature alone, often on the title page. Many collectors prefer this.
- **Inscribed:** signed with a message to a named person ("For Sarah, with best wishes"). Common at readings and signings.
- **Association copy:** inscribed to, or owned by, someone connected to the author or the book's subject: a friend, editor, fellow writer or the real person behind a character. Association copies can be the most valuable of all.
- **Signed limited edition:** part of a numbered edition the author signed in full. Desirable, but signatures are expected, so the premium is built into the edition.
- **Signed bookplate:** a signed label pasted in afterwards. Worth less than a signature on the page, because it isn't tied to that copy.
- **Dated and located signatures:** "signed in London, publication day" can add interest.

## What determines the premium

1. **The author.** A signature from a major author with strong demand adds more than one from a prolific signer whose signed copies are common.
2. **Scarcity of signed copies.** Authors who rarely signed, or died young, have scarcer signatures.
3. **The book itself.** A signature in a first printing of a key work in fine condition is worth far more than the same signature in a late reprint.
4. **Content of the inscription.** Notable recipients, or an inscription that relates to the book, add interest.
5. **Placement.** Title page or half-title is standard; signatures on endpapers are fine; signatures on a separate tipped-in sheet are less desirable.

## Authenticating a signature

Forgeries and printed facsimiles are common in the market for famous names. Before paying a premium:

- **Compare** with verified examples from reputable dealers or auction houses, looking at letterforms, slant and pressure.
- **Inspect** under magnification for genuine pen strokes, indentation and ink variation. Printed signatures are flat and uniform.
- **Check plausibility:** was the author alive and signing when this edition appeared?
- **Look for provenance:** a photo from a signing event, a letter, a receipt or a dealer's guarantee.
- **Buy from professionals** who offer a lifetime guarantee of authenticity for important signatures.

If the price depends on the signature and you can't verify it, assume it's worth only what the unsigned book is worth.

## Things that lower value

- **Facsimile or "autopen" signatures** presented as genuine
- **Signatures by someone other than the author**, such as a previous owner with the same name
- **Erased or altered inscriptions**
- **Signed bookplates in place of a page signature**
- **Poor condition** of the book or jacket

## Selling a signed book

Photograph the signature clearly alongside the title page and copyright page. Describe exactly what it is: flat signed, inscribed (and to whom), or a signed bookplate. Share any provenance.
